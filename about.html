One map, three axes

Most tools show you one thing at a time.

A weather site, an atlas, a news map, a statistics portal — each answers its own question and forgets the others. IntMap puts them on the same Earth, so a question that crosses two of them does not have to cross two websites.

Place

From a street to the whole planet

Flat map, globe, satellite imagery and real 3-D terrain, in one continuous zoom. Tilt into a mountain range and the relief is the measured elevation, not a picture of one.

Data

Layers that stack

Climate, population, energy, trade, railways, cables, hazards, satellites. Put population density over terrain, or a live storm over the infrastructure beneath it — the layers combine instead of replacing one another.

Time

1900 to right now

Move the year and the map moves with it: borders, historical states, population and GDP, bilateral trade, alliance membership and climate periods all follow the same clock.

World news, on the spot where it happened

Recent headlines are placed at the location of the event rather than the newsroom — and you can switch to the newsroom when that is the question. Search it, save articles, read across languages, and see the geography and infrastructure around a story instead of beside it.

Where people are

Population at one-kilometre resolution, GDP per capita, fertility, life expectancy, HDI and more — as a surface over the land, not a colour per country. Put it under a railway network or over terrain and the shape of a region explains itself.

Atlas

Describe it. The map does it.

A hundred layers is a lot of menu. Atlas is IntMap's built-in assistant: say what you want to look at and it moves the camera, switches layers on, selects countries, changes the year and opens the tool you would have gone looking for.

Atlas

Atlas is free once you sign in, up to ten requests a day. It does not yet operate every feature reliably, and complex multi-step requests can fail — so anything that matters is worth checking against its source.

Beyond the map

Keep zooming out. It does not stop at the edge of the world.

There is no button. The same gesture that was zooming the map carries the Earth you were looking at out into the solar system — the same size, the same face, the same instant — and brings it back the same way.

Positions, not decorations The Sun, the eight planets, Pluto and the Moon are placed from published orbital elements, and 177 moons are each propagated in the reference plane their own elements declare.
Stars at their real distances Placed from Hipparcos parallaxes, so pulling far enough away actually changes the constellations. Where a parallax is unusable, the star is reported as a lower bound rather than invented.
The sky from a real place Right-click anywhere for the view from that spot at any date and time, with the horizon computed from the terrain — so mountains hide the stars they actually hide.

Method

A map is only as good as what it admits.

IntMap will tell you what every simulation computes, by which equations and under which assumptions — and where every number came from, under whose licence, and what fetching it means for your privacy. Both are ordinary pages you can read right now, without opening the app.
